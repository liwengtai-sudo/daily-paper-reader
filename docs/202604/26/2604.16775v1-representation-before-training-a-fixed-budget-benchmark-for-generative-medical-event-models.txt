Abstract 

Every prediction from a generative medical event model is bounded by how clinical events are tokenized, yet input representation is rarely isolated from other system and architectural choices. We evaluate how representation decisions affect downstream prediction after a shared one-epoch pretraining budget. We train 28 matched transformers on MIMIC-IV and evaluate them on 30 clinical outcomes in three experiments: (1) quantization granularity, reference-range anchoring, and code-value fusion; (2) value encoding (hard bins, soft discretization, code-normalized xVal) crossed with temporal encoding (event order, time tokens, admission-relative RoPE); and (3) native MIMIC laboratory/vital codes versus the Common Longitudinal ICU Format (CLIF)-remapped laboratory/vital codes with compression-preserving perturbation arms. In Experiment 1, fused code-value tokenization improves mortality AUROC from 0.891 to 0.915 (BH-adjusted p < 0.001), hospital length-of-stay AUROC from 0.763 to 0.788 (BH-adjusted p < 0.001), and, for the decile fused-vs-unfused comparison, mean regression Spearman ρ across the 13 regression outcomes from 0.414 to 0.494. Across the three temporal encodings, event order only and admission-relative RoPE match or exceed inserting time tokens on average while shortening sequences by 11%. CLIF remapping preserves downstream performance in our single-site setting while yielding a smaller, clinically interpretable token set compatible with multi-site use. Finer-than-decile quantization, reference-range anchoring, and soft discretization help in selective outcomes, while code-normalized xVal remains well below the discrete and soft families, consistent with near-median suppression that persists after the affine variant. 

Keywords: Electronic health records, Generative medical event models, Input representa-tion, Clinical decision support, Quantization, Transformer architectures

1. Introduction 

Generative medical event models learn patient representations through self-supervised next-token prediction. Critically, precision lost at tokenization cannot be recovered downstream. However, most benchmarks and model comparisons fix tokenization choices while ablating other parts of the pipeline. Structured health care records mix numeric and categorical attributes. Tokenization determines how numeric magnitude, elapsed time, and event-code vocabulary reach the model. For example, potassium values of 5.0 and 6.5 mEq/L could fall into separate bins or be collapsed into the same coarse category. We ask how such upstream decisions influence downstream performance. We compare performance after a fixed single full pass through the training data, with early stopping disabled and training exposure matched across all configurations. Prior benchmarks stan-dardized tasks or model families rather than the representation layer itself (Fries et al., 2023; Y` eche et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2025). Concurrent work by Guo et al. (2026) studies three binary representation choices in a pediatric setting; we expand the benchmark to quanti-zation granularity, reference-range anchoring, continuous value encoders, and vocabulary perturbation, and evaluate on adult ICU data. A common, standardized vocabulary is one example input representation choice. The Common Longitudinal ICU Format (CLIF: Rojas et al., 2025) is a community-developed schema that harmonizes critical care data across institutions by mapping site-specific laboratory and vital codes to a minimum set of Common ICU data Elements (e.g., LAB/ /50931//mg/dl becomes LAB//glucose_serum ). CLIF was designed for multi-site research; we test whether remapping covered laboratory and vital codes to the smaller shared CLIF vocabulary changes single-site performance, while noting that the same mapping could later support multi-site training through data centralization or federated learning. We organize the benchmark into three experiments with a corresponding question: 1. Experiment 1 Quantization: How should numeric values be quantized, and should code and value share a token? 2. Experiment 2 Value and temporal encoding: How should numeric magnitude and elapsed time be encoded? 3. Experiment 3 Vocabulary semantic grouping: How does remapping laboratory and vital codes to a standardized clinical vocabulary affect model performance? The benchmark defines 30 unique outcomes, with each experiment evaluating 29 because the ICU endpoint differs: Experiments 1–2 use ICU admission, whereas Experiment 3 uses ICU length of stay > 48h. 

Generalizable Insights about Machine Learning in the Context of Healthcare 

Tokenization is a low-overhead design choice in a clinical ML pipeline, but it is often fixed early and never revisited. Our results show that it can shift downstream AUROC substantially. Under the shared one-epoch budget, the benchmark yields four practical findings: First , fuse code and value into a single token . Fused tokenization gives the largest and most consistent gains in our sweep, so it is the strongest default under this budget. 

Second , start with deciles . Finer bins fracture each measurement across more embedding slots, leaving many undertrained under one epoch likely due to the standard loss (cross-entropy) erasing the expected benefit of higher numeric resolution. Third , start without

Replacing covered native MIMIC laboratory and vital codes with CLIF’s expert-curated vocabulary yields a smaller, clinically interpretable token set that remains compatible with the multi-site use case CLIF was designed to support. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. From Recurrent Event Models to Generative Medical Event Models 

In this paper, generative medical event models are self-supervised transformer models trained with next-token prediction over structured clinical event sequences. Before transformer pretraining, longitudinal medical event modeling was often recurrent: Doctor AI (Choi et al., 2016) jointly predicted next-visit diagnoses, medications, and visit timing with recurrent neural networks; RETAIN (Choi et al., 2017) introduced reverse-time recurrent attention for interpretable visit-sequence prediction; and Dipole (Ma et al., 2017) used bidirectional recurrent attention for next-visit diagnosis prediction. Transformer pretraining then expanded to BEHRT (Li et al., 2020) and Med-BERT (Rasmy et al., 2021), which established that self-supervised pretraining on EHR sequences transfers to downstream tasks. More recent studies work with multi-modal event streams: ETHOS-ARES (Renc et al., 2025) pretrains on full MEDS-formatted timelines; EHRMamba (Fallahpour et al., 2024) explores state-space architectures; MOTOR (Steinberg et al., 2023) adds time-to-event pretraining objectives; and Hegselmann et al. (2025) serialize clinical events as text for large language model (LLM) encoding. EHRSHOT (Fries et al., 2023) releases longitudinal structured EHRs, pretrained clinical foundation-model weights, and a shared few-shot evaluation protocol. Despite this breadth, few studies isolate input representation as its own modeling problem. 

2.2. Clinical Data Standards and Interoperability 

Competing clinical coding systems motivate our vocabulary-remapping experiment. The Medical Event Data Standard (MEDS: Medical Event Data Standard Community, 2024) provides the event schema used throughout this study, while the Common Longitudinal ICU Format (CLIF: Rojas et al., 2025) provides a clinically standardized ICU vocabulary. Callahan et al. (2023) show how ontology alignment can be carried out at health-system scale. More directly relevant to model input representation, prior work has tried three main strategies for handling medical codes: text-based code embedding (Hur et al., 2022), subword tokenization adapted to alphanumeric codes such as ICD-10 and SNOMED-CT within multimodal medical foundation models (Dwivedi et al., 2024), and multimodal code tokenization that combines code descriptions with graph context (Su et al., 2025). Whether a standardized vocabulary helps beyond the frequency regularization that comes from collapsing many source codes into fewer shared codes remains open; Experiment 3 tests that question directly ( §4.3).

2.3. Numeric Representation in Transformers 

Encoding continuous values inside discrete-token transformer architectures is an open problem in natural language processing (NLP) (Thawani et al., 2021) and is even more pronounced in clinical data, where numeric precision has direct clinical meaning (e.g., a serum potassium of 5.0 vs. 6.5 mEq/L). Equal-frequency quantile binning is a common starting choice in EHR pipelines because balanced bin frequencies yield uniform token distributions (Renc et al., 2025), but quantile-bin boundaries do not necessarily fall at clinically meaningful thresholds. xVal (Golkar et al., 2024) proposes continuous number tokenization for scientific language models: a placeholder token ( [NUM] ) whose embedding is scaled multiplicatively by a normalized scalar, coupled with an auxiliary numeric regression head. Norouzi et al. (2014) introduced ConSE (Convex Combination of Semantic Embeddings) for zero-shot classification, representing inputs as probability-weighted combinations of category embeddings. We borrow the convex-combination idea and repurpose it for local interpolation between adjacent quantile-bin embeddings, which is distinct from its original setting. Shao et al. (2025) propose continuous autoregressive language models that bypass tokenization entirely. DuETT (Labach et al., 2023) introduces a dual event-time transformer that processes continuous-valued clinical events with a specialized temporal architecture, and Ma et al. (2024) propose temporal cross-attention for dynamic embedding of multimodal EHR data. Our benchmark evaluates hard quantization, soft discretization, and continuous encoders under matched conditions to isolate the effect of numeric precision on downstream performance. 

2.4. Temporal Modeling for Longitudinal Data 

Prior EHR transformers reperesent time in several ways. CEHR-BERT inserts artificial time tokens and combines them with time and age embeddings inside a hybrid structured-EHR transformer (Pang et al., 2021). Time2Vec (Kazemi et al., 2019) learns continuous time features, while MOTOR (Steinberg et al., 2023) uses rotary time embeddings keyed to days since birth inside a time-to-event pretraining objective. More generally, Rotary Position Embeddings encode relative positions through tensor rotations (RoPE: Su et al., 2024). But a recent controlled MIMIC-IV study found no consistent statistically significant benefit from explicit time features across four prediction tasks: in the Triplet representation, each event is encoded as the sum of a code embedding, a time embedding, and a value embedding, and removing the time term ( CVE time ) did not hurt performance consistently; replacing that same time term with Time2Vec or LeTE also did not improve results (Attrach et al., 2025). This result motivates our direct comparison among inserting time tokens, event order alone, and admission-relative rotary position embeddings (admission-relative RoPE) indexed by elapsed time since admission at 1-minute resolution. 

2.5. Benchmarking Efforts 

Existing EHR foundation model benchmarks usually standardize downstream tasks, modal-ities, or model families rather than the tokenization layer itself. EHRSHOT (Fries et al., 2023) evaluates frozen representations on 15 clinical prediction tasks under a shared few-shot protocol. CLMBR (Steinberg et al., 2021) learns patient representations from sequences of daily diagnosis, procedure, medication, and laboratory-order codes, but does not directly model quantitative laboratory values or vitals. HiRID-ICU-Benchmark (Y` eche et al., 2022)

packages nearly 34,000 ICU admissions and 681 high-resolution physiologic variables into a reproducible pipeline for clinically defined time-series prediction tasks. Yu et al. (2025) construct a multimodal MIMIC-IV benchmark at the ICU-stay level that harmonizes hospi-tal, ICU, chest X-ray, and note data, compares eight unimodal and multimodal foundation models, and reports predictive performance, fairness, and interpretability. CLIMB (Dai et al., 2025) expands the benchmark view to 44 datasets and 15 modalities, emphasizing large-scale multimodal pretraining and transfer. EHRStruct (Yang et al., 2025) evaluates 20 LLMs on 11 structured-EHR tasks and analyzes how input format, few-shot prompting, and fine-tuning strategy affect performance. Comparable to our study, Guo et al. (2026) study the representation layer itself. They pretrain eight models that cross three binary choices: whether each clinical event is generated as one fused code-plus-attribute token or as separate code and attribute tokens, whether time is encoded by inserted interval tokens or by RoPE positions derived from patient age, and whether multi-step workflows such as order, collection, and result times are kept as separate events. Across 74 pediatric prediction tasks, fused tokenization consistently outperforms unfused tokenization. Our benchmark further expands the representation choices to quantization granularity, reference-range anchored laboratory binning, continuous value encoders, and vocabulary remapping with compression-preserving perturbations. 

3. Methods 

Overview. Each hospital admission ( hadm id ) is represented as a time-ordered sequence of clinical events. Some events are purely categorical (e.g., transfers or procedures), while others include a numeric measurement (e.g., laboratory or vitals). We focus on the input representation , the pretraining-time procedure that converts heterogeneous time-stamped events into a token sequence that a transformer can consume. 1 The subsequent evaluation uses the first 24 hours of each admission to predict 30 clinical outcomes (17 binary, 13 regression), defined in Table 3. 

3.1. Data Format 

We use MEDS (Medical Event Data Standard Community, 2024)-formatted event streams: each event has subject id , time , code , and optional numeric value . Tokenization produces full-length and first-24h timelines. For soft discretization and code-normalized xVal, the tokenized sequences also retain the underlying numeric values so the model can interpolate between bins or scale the [NUM] token by a code-normalized value.

Numeric values are represented by interpolating between the two adjacent quantile-bin embeddings whose boundary interval contains the value, retaining within-bin position information. We repurpose ConSE’s convex-combination idea (Norouzi et al., 2014) as a local interpolation rule for numeric values rather than as a zero-shot classifier. Values below the first breakpoint or above the last breakpoint use the first or last bin embedding, respectively. Full definitions are shown in Section H.

Temporal Encoding. Three strategies are compared: (i) time tokens between events; (ii) admission-relative RoPE, which removes time tokens and instead uses admission-relative elapsed time converted to 1-minute position IDs to drive rotary position embeddings (Su et al., 2024; Steinberg et al., 2023); and (iii) event order only, which removes time tokens and does not supply admission-relative position IDs, leaving the model to use its default sequential position order. MOTOR (Steinberg et al., 2023) is not considered for the experiments because it changes the pretraining objective, breaking the matched comparison. 

3.3. Vocabulary Semantic Grouping with Compression-Preserving Perturbations 

Experiment 3 isolates the effect of the event-code vocabulary scheme by holding the ICU cohort, model architecture, and training objective fixed across all four arms. All four arms use the discrete value encoder with admission-relative RoPE from Experiment 2. The reported Experiment 3 tokenizer reads only the LAB and VITAL event blocks. The realized input sequence also keeps shared prefix/suffix scaffold tokens for race, language, sex, age, insurance, marital status, admission type, and discharge type; it does not include medication, infusion, transfer, ICU in/out, diagnosis, or procedure tokens (Section J). For all arms, the MEDS event rows, timestamps, and numeric values are held fixed; only the code string changes within the mapped domains. The main comparison is Native MIMIC codes versus CLIF-mapped codes, but that contrast mixes two effects: clinically meaningful regrouping of codes and simpler, more compressed vocabularies. We include two perturbation arms that reuse the mapped-arm target code set: a randomized arm that permutes source-to-target assignments within domain, and a frequency-matched arm that greedily matches mapped-code training frequencies within domain. The full logic is in Section C. 

Vocabulary arms. Native MIMIC leaves all MIMIC code strings unchanged. CLIF-mapped rewrites covered LAB and VITAL codes to CLIF categories while keeping timestamps and numeric values fixed. Because the MEDS code string preserves the unit suffix, one CLIF category can still yield multiple realized token strings (e.g., LAB//albumin//g/dL and 

LAB//albumin//mg/dL ). Randomized mapped codes shuffle the mapped train-split target code list within each code domain and then assign native source codes in sorted order while cycling through that shuffled list as needed. Frequency-matched mapped codes sort native source codes by train-split frequency and greedily assign each one to the mapped target code with the largest remaining train-split count.

Experiment 3 framed data harmonization as the central question: could covered laboratory and vital codes be remapped to a curated shared vocabulary without sacrificing downstream performance? Native MIMIC and CLIF-remapped tokenization performed comparably. On mortality, long hospital length of stay, prolonged ICU stay, and invasive mechanical ventilation, AUROCs were tightly clustered across arms. The mean summaries in Figure 7 were similarly tight: mean binary AUROC was 0 .7751 [0 .7227, 0 .8223] for Native MIMIC codes, 0 .7730 [0 .7237, 0 .8180] for CLIF-mapped, 0 .7728 [0 .7220, 0 .8180] for Frequency-matched mapped codes, and 0 .7688 [0 .7170, 0 .8185] for Randomized mapped codes. 5. Discussion 

Re-evaluating reference-range anchored laboratory binning. Reference-range an-chored laboratory binning places bin boundaries at laboratory normal/abnormal thresholds. Population-based quantization places bin boundaries at population percentiles; a boundary between the 40th and 50th percentile of serum potassium may group normal and critically abnormal values into the same token. Experiment 1 results show no consistent advantage for reference-range anchored laboratory binning over population-quantile binning at matched granularity. At least two factors help explain the null result. First, in laboratory medicine a reference interval is the central 95% of values from a healthy population; it marks normal versus abnormal, not normal versus life-threatening. The binary outcome thresholds in our bench-mark (Table 3) are more extreme than those normal-range cutoffs. Anchoring bins at the normal/abnormal boundary adds resolution around that boundary, but it does not guarantee extra resolution at the severe tails where the binary labels are defined. Centile binning, by contrast, can place bins deeper into the tails simply because each centile covers 1% of the training distribution, so the most extreme 1–2% of values get their own bins regardless of where the normal-range boundary falls. Second, the representation analyses in Section D show that even population-defined bin-token embeddings at high granularity are linearly separable into normal versus abnormal groups: a leave-one-out logistic probe achieves 93–98% accuracy on centile embeddings. Un-der frozen representations, the downstream classifier learns clinically defined thresholds even when bins are population-defined. The probe result supports decodability, not unsupervised recovery of the clinical boundary during pretraining. 

Vocabulary sparsity and the granularity paradox. Quantization granularities finer than deciles yield diminishing or negative returns relative to deciles (Tables 1 and 2 and fig. 4). Finer granularities may converge with additional training data or epochs; we therefore inter-pret the result narrowly: deciles are the best broad starting configuration in the current benchmark, not an intrinsic representational optimum. Fine-grained binning fractures com-mon measurements across hundreds of distinct tokens, leaving individual token embeddings under-trained (e.g., stepping from deciles to centiles under fused tokenization introduces nearly 25,000 zero-frequency tokens into the 83,927-token embedding matrix). For rounded laboratory values, granularity can also exceed effective granularity: potassium centiles collapse to only 27 unique train-split thresholds, so the fused centile tokenizer realizes 28 potassium bins instead of the full 100. Fused settings pay this sparsity penalty more sharply because each code carries its own value-token set. Sequence-length savings are unlikely to be the main explanation for the fused-token advantage, because > 99 .95% of first-24h admissions fit within the 4096-token context window under both fused and unfused decile tokenizations (Figure 10). 

Value encoding. Why does soft discretization win on only 7 of 29 outcomes? One possible explanation is that the convex-combination mechanism still injects a continuous, out-of-vocabulary input vector into a discrete autoregressive prediction problem: the model conditions on an interpolated embedding that has no exact match in the embedding table, and the output side can only approximate that continuity with a local two-bin soft target at

quantile-token positions (Section D). For xVal, near-median suppression further limits the injected signal because z ≈ 0 collapses the multiplicative scaling term; the affine shift does not recover performance. Natural follow-ups include fused soft discretization, richer local mixtures of nearby bin embeddings, and code-conditioned continuous injections, but those extensions increase model size and may confound representation comparisons. 

Temporal encoding. Both the event-order-only and admission-relative-RoPE arms omit inserting time tokens, which shortens sequences by about 11% (median 83 vs. 93 tokens per 24h admission for unfused deciles; Figure 11). The remaining difference between those two arms is whether position IDs carry admission-relative minute-resolution timestamps. The inserted-time-token arm discretizes elapsed gaps into 13 coarse spacing bins (from 5 minutes to 6 months or more) and uses extra sequence slots for those tokens. Within the discrete family, the largest outcome-level gaps between event order only and inserted time tokens appear on electrolyte and chemistry outcomes such as minimum sodium Spearman ρ (0 .502 vs. 0 .467), maximum sodium (0 .454 vs. 0 .426), and peak creatinine (0 .586 vs. 0 .564). A concurrent MIMIC-IV ablation (Attrach et al., 2025) likewise found no statistically significant benefit from explicit time features, consistent with our results. A plausible explanation is that inserted time tokens both consume token budget and represent elapsed time more coarsely than admission-relative RoPE, which preserves minute-resolution relative timing without lengthening the sequence. 

Limitations. Single-site evaluation (MIMIC-IV), one model family, one training seed, and a fixed model scale are the primary constraints on scope. The 4096-token cap reduces truncation to < 0.05% of first-24h admissions but cannot accommodate long-tail timelines entirely. The one-epoch budget enforces equal data exposure across configurations, but it does not characterize late-stage convergence; some representation choices may separate differently under larger compute budgets. The last-hidden-state probe may also disadvantage representations that distribute information across multiple tokens, and we do not evaluate alternative pooling strategies or fine-tuned downstream heads. Multi-site validation and scaling experiments remain open. 

6. Conclusion 

Input representation is typically treated as fixed plumbing in generative medical event models. ETHOS (Renc et al., 2025), CLMBR-T-base (Fries et al., 2023), and Curiosity (Waxler et al., 2025) all discretized numeric laboratory values into ten population quantiles without comprehensive justification. Under a matched one-epoch pretraining budget on MIMIC-IV, we show that those choices shift downstream performance substantially. Four results are practical: fusing code and value into a single token produced the largest and most consistent gains (e.g., mortality AUROC 0.891 to 0.915); deciles outperformed finer granularities, which fracture measurements across undertrained embedding slots; temporal encodings that omit explicit time tokens (event order, admission-relative RoPE) matched or exceeded time-token insertion while shortening sequences by roughly 11%; and remapping covered laboratory and vital codes to the CLIF vocabulary preserved single-site performance while yielding a smaller, cross-site-compatible token set. These findings are bound by a single site, one model family, one seed, and one training epoch. Multi-site validation, larger compute budgets,

and extensions such as fused soft discretization or code-conditioned continuous injection remain open. Upstream representation is not a simple preprocessing detail: it sets a ceiling on what the model can learn from EHR sequences, and revisiting it is among the cheapest meaningful interventions available before scaling the model or data.

Appendix C. Experiment 3 Perturbation Logic 

The main-text comparison between Native MIMIC codes and CLIF-mapped codes mixes at least two mechanisms: semantic grouping , where clinically related MIMIC codes are reassigned to the same mapped concept (e.g., LAB//50931//mg/dl → LAB//glucose_serum ); and vocabulary compression , where multiple MIMIC codes collapse into fewer mapped identifiers so each learned token appears more often during the fixed single training pass. The randomized and frequency-matched perturbation arms reuse the same mapped train-split target code set; codes without a CLIF mapping retain their native MIMIC strings. 

Nested-set definition. To separate those mechanisms conceptually, we define three nested classes of mappings: 

• A: clinically principled mappings, i.e., the CLIF semantic groupings encoded by the CLIF-MIMIC tables; 

• B: mappings over the mapped code set that match how often each mapped code appears in the training split but need not preserve the original clinical groupings; 

• C: mappings over the mapped code set that match a comparable level of vocabulary compression, regardless of exactly how often each mapped code appears. Conceptually, A ⊂ B ⊂ C. A direct comparison between native MIMIC codes and CLIF-mapped codes only tells us whether moving from outside C to a point in A changes performance; it does not identify which part of that move matters. 

Interpretation logic. The nested sets imply a qualitative decision rule. If a perturbation arm from C \ B matches the mapped arm, coarse vocabulary compression is sufficient. If such a perturbation arm falls short but a perturbation arm from B \ A matches the mapped arm, then matching mapped-code frequencies is sufficient. Only if both perturbations fall short do we attribute residual gains to the specific semantic grouping encoded by A. In the implementation, the randomized arm shuffles the mapped target code list once within each domain and assigns sorted native source codes while cycling through that shuffled list as needed, whereas the frequency-matched arm sorts native source codes by descending train-split frequency and greedily assigns each one to the mapped target code with the largest remaining train-split count. Because those perturbations only approximate the hierarchy, we apply this logic qualitatively, not as an exact set-membership test.

D.1. Embedding Geometry: Shared vs. Code-Specific Centile Encoders 

To visualize the effect of code-value fusion on the numeric embedding manifold, we project the centile-token embeddings to two dimensions with PCA (Figure 8). Under unfused tokenization, every measurement reuses the same shared quantile-token embeddings rather than owning code-specific value tokens. Under fusion, each variable owns a distinct arc of code-specific centile tokens, and the realized bin counts are typically well below the nominal granularity for rounded laboratory values (e.g., potassium collapses to 28 of the nominal 100 centiles).

D.2. Linear Decodability of Clinical Boundaries 

We test whether clinically defined normal/abnormal labels are linearly decodable from learned bin-token embeddings. We use the population-quantile, unfused discrete models from Experiment 1 (time tokens) across four granularities (deciles/ventiles/trentiles/centiles). For seven lab tests, we label each bin as normal or abnormal based on whether the bin midpoint falls within the MIMIC-IV reference interval for that test, then fit a leave-one-out logistic regression probe using the corresponding static token embeddings (one example per bin). Centile embeddings achieve 93–98% accuracy across all seven tests (Figure 9a). Decile and ventile embeddings score lower (70–90%), consistent with coarser bins spanning both sides of the threshold. Because the probe is supervised on reference-range labels, this accuracy should be interpreted as separability : pretraining induces bin embeddings that a linear classifier can use to fit a clinically defined boundary. It does not imply that the transformer inferred the clinical threshold during pretraining.

D.4. Impact of Out-of-Vocabulary Encodings 

Soft discretization creates interpolated embedding vectors that lie between the vocabulary’s discrete token embeddings. When next-token prediction is evaluated using a one-hot cross-entropy loss, the model conditions on a continuous input vector with no exact match in the embedding table. Because Experiment 3 evaluates a categorical vocabulary intervention, we use the discrete value encoder with admission-relative RoPE ( §4.2) rather than soft discretization as the baseline.

Appendix G. Tokenized Sequence Length Distributions 

Packed training boundary marker (Poisson PAD gaps). In packed training, we concatenate tokenized admissions into fixed-length blocks for efficient use. To reduce accidental cross-admission continuity, we insert a short run of PAD tokens between admissions, with the number of PAD tokens drawn from a Poisson distribution with mean ≈ 7 (Burkhart et al., 2025, 2026). 

Appendix H. Formal Definitions of Value Encoders 

The following definitions formalize the soft and continuous value encoders used in Experi-ment 2 (the discrete encoder is the standard next-token cross-entropy setup). 

ConSE-variant soft discretization. Let v be a numeric value and let bi ≤ v < b i+1 be the adjacent quantile boundaries that surround it. We compute an interpolation weight 

α = ( v − bi)/(bi+1 − bi) (with the convention that α = 0 if bi+1 = bi) and represent the value by interpolating between the two adjacent shared quantile-bin embeddings whose boundary interval contains v:

e(v) = (1 − α) Ei + α Ei+1 .

At quantile-token prediction positions, we use the same α to replace the one-hot next-token target with a two-point distribution over the adjacent quantile tokens Qi and Qi+1 :

Lsoft (v) = −(1 − α) log p(Qi | · ) − α log p(Qi+1 | · ) .

All non-quantile targets retain the standard causal language-model cross-entropy. For values below the first stored breakpoint or above the last stored breakpoint, we use the first or last quantile-bin embedding rather than extrapolating beyond the boundary range.

Appendix J. Experiment 3 CLIF Mapping Scope 

The tokenizer used for the reported Experiment 3 runs reads the LAB and VITAL event blocks, together with shared admission/discharge and demographic scaffold tokens for race, language, sex, age, insurance, marital status, admission type, and discharge type. The reported Experiment 3 models should therefore be interpreted as a LAB/VITAL-only vocabulary-remapping study, not as a full MEDS or full-CLIF harmonization experiment. The arm builder rewrites CLIF-covered LAB, VITAL, MEDICATION, and INFUSION code families upstream, but the reported tokenizer excludes medication, infusion, transfer, ICU in/out, diagnosis, procedure, and respiratory-support-like event families from the model input. Within that realized scope, the CLIF mapping reaches the model through: 

• LAB : the train vocabulary rewrites 100 distinct native LAB code strings spanning 95 MIMIC itemid s into 62 CLIF-style LAB token strings across 46 lab category values; the train split maps 11 344 725/19 075 800 LAB events (59.5%). 

• VITAL : the train vocabulary rewrites 26 distinct native VITAL code strings spanning 26 MIMIC itemid s into 13 CLIF-style VITAL token strings across 10 vital category 

values; the train split maps 28 283 881/202 102 025 VITAL events (14.0%).
